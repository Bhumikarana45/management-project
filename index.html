Evolution of Management

From ancient civilisations to modern management: how the way we organise people and resources has changed, who shaped each stage, and what each one still teaches us.

Five stagesOther schoolsTodayCompareQuiz

What is management?

Management is the process of getting things done through people, using resources efficiently (with the least waste) and effectively (reaching the right goals). Every manager plans, organises, leads and controls. The ideas behind those four functions took thousands of years to develop.

Five stages

  1. 1

    Early Management

    Ancient civilisations to the 1800s

    People managed large projects long before management had a name. Without written theory, leaders still had to plan, divide work and keep thousands of people coordinated.

    • Egypt: the pyramids needed planning, labour organisation and supply of materials over decades
    • China: the Great Wall was built and maintained across dynasties
    • Rome: the army used a clear chain of command and standard units
    • India: Kautilya's Arthashastra described administration, taxation and the duties of officials

    The Industrial Revolution (late 1700s–1800s)

    Factories replaced work done at home. Owners now managed hundreds of workers and machines, so they needed rules, supervision and a better way to organise work. Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) showed how division of labour raised output.

    Key concept: Organisation & Coordination
  2. 2

    Scientific Management

    1911 · F. W. Taylor, "Father of Scientific Management"

    Taylor, an American engineer, believed there was one best way to do every job, and that it could be found by careful study instead of guesswork. He set out his ideas in The Principles of Scientific Management (1911).

    Principles

    • Science, not rule of thumb
    • Harmony, not discord
    • Cooperation, not individualism
    • Develop each worker to their greatest efficiency

    Techniques

    • Time study and motion study
    • Standardisation of tools and methods
    • Differential piece-wage system
    • Functional foremanship

    Criticism: it treated workers like machines and ignored their feelings and social needs.

    Others in this school

    Frank and Lillian Gilbreth refined motion study. Henry Gantt created the Gantt chart, still used to plan projects.

    Key concept: Efficiency
  3. 3

    Administrative Management

    1916 · Henri Fayol, "Father of Modern Management"

    Fayol, a French mining engineer, looked at management from the top of the organisation. In General and Industrial Management (1916) he said management is a skill that can be taught, and named five functions: planning, organising, commanding, coordinating and controlling.

    Fayol's 14 principles

    1. Division of work
    2. Authority and responsibility
    3. Discipline
    4. Unity of command
    5. Unity of direction
    6. Subordination of individual interest to general interest
    7. Remuneration
    8. Centralisation
    9. Scalar chain
    10. Order
    11. Equity
    12. Stability of tenure
    13. Initiative
    14. Esprit de corps

    Max Weber: bureaucratic management

    The German sociologist Max Weber described the ideal bureaucracy: a clear hierarchy, written rules, jobs filled on merit, and impersonal, fair treatment. Governments and large companies still use these ideas.

    Key concept: Management Functions
  4. 4

    Human Relations Approach

    1930s · Elton Mayo and the Hawthorne Studies

    The Hawthorne Studies (1924–1932) at Western Electric's plant near Chicago began as a test of how lighting affected output. Productivity rose even when the lights were dimmed. Mayo's team concluded that attention, recognition and the work group mattered more than physical conditions.

    • Workers are social beings, not just economic ones
    • Informal groups set their own norms
    • Good communication and supervision raise morale
    • Being noticed can change behaviour (the "Hawthorne effect")

    The behavioural approach (1940s–1960s)

    • Abraham Maslow (1943): hierarchy of needs, from physiological to self-actualisation
    • Frederick Herzberg (1959): motivators (achievement, recognition) versus hygiene factors (pay, conditions)
    • Douglas McGregor (1960): Theory X assumes people avoid work; Theory Y assumes they seek responsibility
    Key concept: People & Motivation
  5. 5

    Modern Management

    1980s – Present

    Modern management combines the earlier schools and adds a focus on quality, strategy, global competition and constant change.

    • Management by Objectives: Peter Drucker (1954); managers and staff agree on goals together
    • Total Quality Management: W. Edwards Deming; continuous improvement and customer focus
    • Theory Z (1981): William Ouchi; lessons from Japanese firms such as long-term employment and group decisions
    • Strategic management: planning for long-term competitive advantage
    • Globalisation, technology and sustainability
    Key concept: Innovation & Adaptability

Three more schools (1940s–1970s)

Between the human relations era and modern management, three approaches changed how managers think.

Quantitative approach

1940s · grew out of World War II

  • Operations research and statistics
  • Linear programming, forecasting, inventory models
  • Decisions based on data and models

Systems approach

1950s–1960s · Chester Barnard, Ludwig von Bertalanffy

  • An organisation is a system of connected parts
  • Inputs → processes → outputs → feedback
  • A change in one part affects the whole

Contingency approach

1960s–1970s · Fred Fiedler, Joan Woodward, Burns & Stalker

  • There is no one best way to manage
  • The right method depends on the situation
  • Size, technology and environment all matter

Management today

  • Used in business, education, healthcare, government and non-profit organisations
  • AI and data analytics support faster, better decisions
  • Remote and hybrid teams across countries
  • Agile, flexible structures instead of rigid hierarchy
  • Sustainability, ethics and social responsibility (ESG)

Key takeaways

  • Planning
  • Organising
  • Leading
  • Controlling
  • Innovation
  • People

Conclusion

Management has moved from traditional ways of organising work to scientific methods, employee-focused practices, data and systems thinking, and modern technology. No single approach replaced the others. Today's managers borrow from all of them: Taylor's efficiency, Fayol's functions, Mayo's attention to people, and the contingency view that the right answer depends on the situation.

Better management, a brighter future.
Good management builds great organisations.
